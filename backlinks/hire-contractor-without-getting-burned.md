**Title:** The cheapest bid cost me $11,000 more. What do you insist on before signing with a contractor?

Back in 2019 I hired a tile setter for a duplex unit in Toledo. He was $2,400 under the other two bids, showed up at 7 a.m. sharp, and seemed like exactly the kind of guy you want on a job. By the time everything was sorted out, that cheap bid had cost me about $11,000 more than the bid I passed on.

The lesson wasn't "don't hire cheap contractors." It was that I gave up control of the money and the paperwork because I liked the person.

What I do now on every job:

- Payment schedule tied to finished, inspected work, not dates or "progress"
- Small deposit for materials only, never half up front
- Written scope that names materials, brands, and who handles demo and disposal
- Change orders in writing, with a price, before the work happens
- Lien waivers collected with each payment
- License and insurance verified with the issuer, not from a photo of a card

Reviews tell you someone is pleasant. A payment schedule tells you what happens when they aren't.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I avoid getting ripped off by a contractor?

I'm a project manager and I've hired a lot of trades. The honest answer is that you protect yourself with money and paperwork, not with your instincts about the person.

I learned that the expensive way. In 2019 I hired a tile setter for a duplex unit in Toledo because he was $2,400 under the other two bids and showed up at 7 a.m. sharp. He was friendly, punctual and confident. That job ended up costing me about $11,000 more than taking a higher bid would have. Nothing about him in person predicted it.

Here is what actually protects you:

1. Tie payments to finished work. Pay a small deposit for materials, then release each payment only when a defined stage is complete and you've inspected it. Never pay ahead of the work. If the contractor walks off, you've only paid for what exists.

2. Get a written scope with specifics: materials, brands, quantities, who handles demo and disposal, and what "done" looks like. Cheap bids make their money back in vague scopes.

3. Put every change order in writing, with a price, before the work happens.

4. Collect lien waivers with each payment, so a supplier or sub the contractor didn't pay can't come after your property.

5. Verify the license and insurance directly with the issuing agency or the insurer. A photo of a certificate proves nothing.

6. Compare bids line by line. When one comes in far below the others, find out exactly what's missing from it before you call it a deal.

Reviews and gut feel show you how someone behaves when things go well. Your contract and payment schedule decide what happens when they don't.
