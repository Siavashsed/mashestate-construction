**Title:** I took the lowest of three kitchen bids and it ended up costing me more than the highest. What should I have looked for?

A few years back I got three bids to gut a kitchen in a duplex I rent out in Dayton: $41,200, $47,800 and $52,500. I took the cheapest and felt pretty good about it for about nine days. Then the change orders started. By the end it was $9,400 over, which put me above where the mid-range bid would have landed, and I'd lost weeks to the delays.

What I learned afterward is that the total at the bottom isn't the useful part of a bid. The useful part is what's missing. When I laid the three bids side by side, the low one had vague allowances for cabinets and flooring, no line for permits, nothing about demolition debris removal, and no mention of who handles electrical or plumbing rough-in changes. The other two spelled those out. The low bidder wasn't cheaper, just quieter about the costs.

Now I compare scope line by line before I even look at the number, and I ask every contractor what is NOT included.

Curious what other people check first when bids come in. Any red flags you've learned the hard way?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I compare contractor bids and spot red flags before hiring someone for a renovation?

The biggest mistake I see, and one I made myself, is starting with the total. The number at the bottom of a bid is a sales tool. The real information is in what's missing.

Here's the process I use now as a project manager and landlord.

1. Get at least three bids, and make sure every contractor is bidding the same scope. If they aren't, you're comparing apples to fence posts.

2. Put the bids side by side and go line by line. Look for categories that appear in one bid and not the others: permits, demolition and debris removal, electrical and plumbing rough-in, drywall repair, painting, and cleanup.

3. Watch for vague allowances. If a bid says "cabinets: allowance" with no dollar figure or spec, the contractor can come back later and say your choice exceeded it. That's how a cheap bid turns into change orders.

4. Ask what is NOT included. Make them answer in writing. Good contractors answer easily. Evasive answers tell you something too.

5. Ask how change orders are priced and approved. You want a written process, with markup stated up front, before work begins.

6. Check payment terms. A large deposit before any materials are ordered is a warning sign.

I learned this the hard way. In 2022 I got three bids to gut a kitchen in a rental duplex: $41,200, $47,800 and $52,500. I took the lowest and ended up $9,400 over in change orders, which put me past the mid-range bid, with delays on top.
