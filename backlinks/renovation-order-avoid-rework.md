**Title:** What's the right order for a gut renovation? I learned the expensive way that tile before wiring is a $4,200 mistake

I bought a 1962 duplex as a rental a few years back, and I want to share a mistake so nobody else repeats it. We finished a bathroom completely: new tile, fixtures, paint, tenant-ready. Then three weeks later we found that the wiring behind that wall wasn't up to code and the plumbing needed to be rerouted. Everything we'd just finished had to come out. The rework cost me about $4,200.

The sequence I follow now is: test for hazards first (asbestos, lead, mold, old wiring), then demo, then rough-in everything behind the walls (electrical, plumbing, HVAC), then get it inspected, then close it up, and only then finish from the top down. Ceilings first, then walls, then floors.

The rule I use: anything that gets closed up before it's been tested is a future demolition job. In an older house especially, you can't trust what you can't see.

Curious what order others have used, and whether anyone has found a better way to handle the inspection step. Did you ever have to rip out finished work?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is the correct order to renovate a house to avoid expensive rework?

The short version: test, demo, rough-in, inspect, close up, then finish from the top down. The principle behind it is that anything hidden behind a wall should be tested and inspected before the wall is closed.

Here is the sequence I use as a construction writer and project manager:

1. Test for hazards first. In older homes this means asbestos, lead paint, mold, and questionable wiring. Finding these after demo or finishing changes your budget and schedule.

2. Demolition. Take things down to the studs where needed so you can see what you're actually working with.

3. Rough-in everything behind the walls. Electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and any structural fixes happen now, all before anything is covered. Doing these trades in the wrong order is a common source of rework, because one trade often has to cut into another's finished work.

4. Inspect. Get the rough-in inspected and corrected while it's still exposed. Fixing a problem at this stage costs very little. Fixing it later means demolition.

5. Close it up. Insulation, drywall, and taping come after the inspection passes.

6. Finish from the top down. Ceilings first, then walls, then trim, and floors last. Gravity is not on your side otherwise: paint drips onto finished floors and tile gets scratched by later trades.

I learned this the hard way on a 1962 duplex I bought as a rental. We finished a bathroom completely, tile and all, and three weeks later had to tear it out because the wiring and plumbing behind it hadn't been tested. That mistake cost me about $4,200.

My rule is that anything closed up before it's been tested is a future demolition job. If you want the longer version, I wrote it up here: https://siavashsed.github.io/mashestate-construction/renovation-order-avoid-rework/
