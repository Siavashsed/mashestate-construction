**Title:** Anyone else's renovation quote blow up the minute the drywall came off?

Bought a 1960s duplex in March 2019 and got a $38,000 quote for the bathroom and kitchen. Felt reasonable. Then on a cold Tuesday morning at 7:15 we pulled the first sheet of drywall and found a rotted sill plate. By the time everything was done we were at $51,400.

What I took from it: the quote wasn't dishonest. Nobody can price what they can't see. A contractor pricing the visible work is giving you a number for the part of the house that's showing, and the part behind the walls is a blank. In an older house that blank is usually where the money goes: old plumbing, knob-and-tube wiring, water damage, framing that was never up to code.

So now I treat the estimate as the floor, not the total. I add a separate contingency line, and for a house this age I'd say 20 to 30 percent, held back and not allocated to anything fun like nicer tile. If it doesn't get used, great.

Curious what others have done. How much contingency do you carry, and what's the worst thing you found behind a wall?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why do renovation costs almost always end up higher than the original estimate?

Short answer: the estimate prices what can be seen, and the expensive problems are usually the ones that can't be seen yet.

When a contractor quotes a kitchen or bathroom remodel, they're pricing the visible scope: demolition, new fixtures, finishes, labor. What they can't price is the condition of everything behind the walls and under the floors. Until it's opened up, that's a blank, and in practice it gets filled in with an optimistic assumption of zero problems.

I learned this firsthand. I bought a 1960s duplex in March 2019 with a $38,000 quote for a bathroom and kitchen. On a cold Tuesday morning we pulled the first sheet of drywall and found a rotted sill. The final cost was $51,400. The contractor wasn't lying or lowballing. The number was honest for what was knowable at the time.

A few things make overruns more likely:

1. Age of the house. Older homes hide outdated wiring, galvanized or cast iron plumbing, moisture damage and framing that doesn't meet current code.
2. Chain reactions. One discovery, like rot, triggers others: structural repair, then permits, then re-finishing work that was already priced.
3. Scope creep. Once walls are open, it's tempting to upgrade things while you're in there.
4. Material and schedule changes that stretch the timeline.

What works is to stop treating the estimate as the total. Treat it as the floor, and add a separate contingency line of roughly 15 to 20 percent for newer homes and 25 to 30 percent for older ones. Keep it unallocated. If you don't need it, you've saved money. If you do, it's not a crisis.
