**Title:** If your walls are open for a reno, air sealing pays back faster than windows or solar. Has anyone found otherwise?

Something I keep seeing in renovation threads: people get window quotes or a solar estimate first and leave the attic for "someday." In my experience that order is backwards.

In the winter of 2023 I gutted the kitchen in a 1962 ranch duplex I rent out. With the drywall already open, I sealed the top plates, the wire penetrations and the plumbing chase. It took an afternoon and a few tubes of foam and caulk. Then I weatherstripped and insulated the attic hatch and added insulation up top.

This is the payback order I've landed on for upgrades done during a reno:
1. Air sealing (cheapest and fastest)
2. Attic insulation
3. Heat pump water heater, especially if the old tank is near end of life
4. Windows and rooftop solar near the bottom, unless the windows are actually failing

The big factor is timing. Open walls are the cheapest chance you'll ever get to air seal a house, and once the drywall goes back up that chance is gone.

I'd like to hear from anyone whose numbers came out differently, especially in hot climates where cooling dominates. I wrote up my full reasoning on my site.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Which energy-efficient upgrades pay for themselves the fastest during a home renovation?

In almost every house I've worked on, the answer is air sealing first, attic insulation second, and a heat pump water heater close behind. New windows and rooftop solar usually come near the bottom on payback, whatever the salesperson at the home show tells you.

The reason is cost against impact. Air leaks around top plates, wire and plumbing penetrations, recessed lights and the attic hatch let heated or cooled air escape all day. Sealing them takes caulk, foam and labor, and the labor is nearly free when the walls are already open. Attic insulation works the same way: it's cheap per square foot and covers the surface that loses the most heat.

A heat pump water heater makes the most sense when your old tank is near the end of its life anyway. Then you're only paying the difference over a standard replacement, not the full price.

Windows and solar aren't bad investments. They just cost a lot for what they save. Replace windows that are rotted or failing, but energy savings alone rarely justify them. Solar depends heavily on your utility rates, incentives and roof, and it works better on a house that has already been sealed and insulated, since you can size a smaller system.

I learned this on my own rental. In the winter of 2023 I gutted the kitchen in a 1962 ranch duplex. With the drywall open, sealing and insulating took an afternoon and a few tubes of foam, and nothing else in that project paid back as quickly. My practical advice: if any walls or ceilings are going to be open during your renovation, plan the air sealing before you sign off on anything else.
