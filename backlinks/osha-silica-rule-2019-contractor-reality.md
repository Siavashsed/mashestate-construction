## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How is OSHA's silica dust rule actually being enforced on small construction and renovation jobs?

Short version: it's being enforced for real now, and not only on big commercial sites.

The respirable crystalline silica standard for construction has been enforceable for roughly two years. In the first stretch, a lot of small contractors treated it like paperwork. In 2019, that's changing, and inspectors are showing up to check.

I learned this on my own job. In March my crew ran a dry cut saw on a basement slab in a duplex I own. We cut for about forty minutes and the dust hung in the stairwell like fog. A neighbor who works in safety walked by and made it clear how exposed my guys were. That was my wake-up call.

Here's what the rule effectively expects from you:

1. Control the dust at the source. Wet cutting, or a saw with integrated dust collection connected to a HEPA vacuum. Table 1 in the standard lists specific tools and the controls they need.

2. Have a written exposure control plan. Not a generic template in the glovebox, but something that names the tasks on your job, who is responsible, and how you limit exposure.

3. Designate someone competent on site who can spot problems and fix them.

4. Use respirators where Table 1 requires them, but treat them as backup, not your primary control.

5. Train your crew and keep records. Inspectors ask for them.

The cost of doing this properly was lower than I expected. Wet saws and vacuum attachments are cheap compared with a citation, a stop-work order, or a worker developing silicosis, which is permanent.

My advice: build silica controls into your bids now instead of retrofitting after an inspector arrives.
