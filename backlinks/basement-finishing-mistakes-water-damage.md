**Title:** Is the vapor barrier behind finished basement walls causing more rot than it prevents?

I've torn out a lot of finished basements, and the same thing keeps coming up: most of the damage isn't from a flood. The finish job itself causes it.

The usual culprit is 6-mil poly stapled behind the studs against the foundation wall. The idea is to keep moisture out. But concrete wicks ground moisture all the time, and the poly holds it against the framing. The wall can't dry inward or outward. Five or six years later the bottom plate is as soft as wet cardboard, and nobody noticed because the drywall looked fine.

Last March I opened a wall in one of my own rentals, a 1962 split-level I bought from a flipper, and found exactly that: fiberglass batts, poly, and an untreated plate sitting on bare slab.

What has worked better for me is rigid foam glued and taped straight to the concrete, framing inside it, a pressure-treated bottom plate on a sill gasket, and no interior poly.

Has anyone else opened a wall and found the same thing? Or has anyone had a poly install that actually held up?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Should I put a plastic vapor barrier behind the studs when finishing my basement walls?

In most cases, no. In my experience it's one of the most common reasons finished basements rot from the inside.

The logic sounds reasonable: basements are damp, so you add plastic to keep the damp out. The problem is that a foundation wall is never truly dry. Concrete is porous and keeps wicking moisture from the soil behind it. When you staple 6-mil poly behind the studs, that moisture condenses and collects in the framing cavity with no way out. The plastic blocks drying toward the room, and the concrete blocks drying toward the outside. The wall stays wet.

It happens slowly, which is why it fools people. The drywall looks fine for years. Then you pull it off and find the bottom plate as soft as wet cardboard. Last March I opened a wall in one of my own rentals, a 1962 split-level I'd bought from a flipper, and found exactly that: fiberglass batts, poly and an untreated plate on bare slab, five or six years into a slow rot.

Here is what I do instead:

1. Fix the water first. Check the grading, gutters, downspout extensions and any active leaks before you frame anything.
2. Put rigid foam (XPS or foil-faced polyiso) directly against the concrete, with the seams taped. The foam controls vapor and keeps the inside face warm enough to prevent condensation.
3. Frame inside the foam, using a pressure-treated bottom plate on a sill gasket.
4. Skip the interior poly so the assembly can dry toward the room.
5. Use mineral wool or unfaced batts in the stud cavity if you need more insulation.

Local code varies, so check with your inspector. But the principle holds everywhere: a basement wall has to be able to dry.
