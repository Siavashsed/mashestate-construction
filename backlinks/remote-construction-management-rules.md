**Title:** Managing a gut reno from 700 miles away taught me the software was never the problem. What actually worked?

I ran a 1,900 sq ft gut renovation on a Tulsa duplex from my kitchen table last March, about 700 miles away. The first three weeks felt great. I was texting daily with the framer, getting photos, and everything looked on schedule.

The problem is that photos and texts show you what someone chooses to show you. By week four I realized the photos were carefully framed and the actual progress was well behind what I was being told.

Two things fixed it. First, I put someone I trusted physically on site every working day, even a part-time project manager or an inspector-type person, because a weekly walkthrough is too slow to catch problems. Second, I stopped paying against promises and started paying against what that person verified in person. Draws only went out after someone confirmed the work was actually done and done correctly.

When the contractor knows payment depends on independent verification, the whole conversation changes. Disputes get shorter and surprises get smaller.

Curious how others handle this when they can't be on site. Anyone found a setup that works better than a paid on-site rep?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Can you manage a construction or renovation project remotely, and what are the rules to make it work?

Yes, you can, but software is not what makes it work. The rule I'd put above all others: someone you trust must be physically on site every working day, and you should pay against what they verify, not what the contractor promises.

I learned this on a 1,900 square foot gut renovation of a Tulsa duplex that I ran from my kitchen table 700 miles away in March 2024. The first three weeks went fine, largely because I was texting with a framer I trusted and receiving regular photos. Then the gaps showed up. Photos show what someone chooses to frame, and a text saying 'done' can mean anything from finished to started.

Here is what I'd do if starting again.

1. Hire an on-site representative. This can be a part-time project manager, a trusted local relative or a third-party inspector. Weekly visits are too slow. Problems in framing, plumbing rough-in or electrical get expensive fast once drywall goes up.

2. Tie every payment to verified progress. Use a draw schedule with defined milestones. No one gets paid for work that your representative hasn't confirmed in person, with dated photos or video as backup.

3. Keep a written scope and change-order process. Verbal changes are where remote owners lose the most money.

4. Set a fixed communication rhythm. A short daily update and a weekly call beat constant texting.

5. Keep your tools simple. A shared folder, a spreadsheet and a scheduling app are plenty.

I wrote up the full set of rules and the mistakes I made here, if you want more detail: https://siavashsed.github.io/mashestate-construction/remote-construction-management-rules/

The short version: trust, but verify, and let the person who is standing in the building be the one who verifies.
