**Title:** Living in the house during a gut reno: plan for the dust before you plan the cabinets

Back in 2019 I gutted the kitchen in the lower unit of a 1962 ranch duplex while living upstairs. I had spent months on cabinet and counter choices. I spent about ten minutes thinking about how we would actually live through it. That was a big mistake.

Here is what I would do differently, and what I tell people now:

- Set up dust containment before demo day, not after the first mess. That means plastic sheeting with taped seams, a zippered door, and tape over the HVAC returns in the work zone.
- Pull air out of the work area. A real air scrubber is best, but even a box fan blowing out a window helps a lot.
- Pick one room that stays clean and usable no matter what. Coffee, a microwave, somewhere to sit. No crew, no boots.
- Shut off the HVAC during dusty work or filter it well, and change the filters often.
- In older homes, test for lead and asbestos before anyone swings a hammer.

That one working room did more for our sanity than anything else.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do you live in your house during a major renovation without the dust taking over?

I am a construction writer and project manager, and I have also learned this one the hard way. In 2019 I gutted the kitchen in the lower unit of my duplex, a 1962 ranch, while living upstairs. I planned the cabinets for months and planned how we would live for about ten minutes. The dust was in every room within days.

Here is what actually works.

1. Contain the work zone before demo starts. Hang plastic sheeting from floor to ceiling, tape the seams, add a zippered entry and put sticky mats at the door. Seal the HVAC returns and supply registers in the work area with plastic and tape.

2. Create negative air pressure. A proper air scrubber is ideal. Even a box fan blowing out a window in the work room pulls dust away from the rest of the house instead of pushing it in.

3. Manage the HVAC. Either shut it off during dusty work or use a higher MERV filter and change it often. Otherwise your ductwork carries the dust to every room.

4. Protect one working room. Pick one space that stays clean no matter what, with a coffee maker, a microwave and somewhere to sit. The crew stays out of it. It sounds minor, but it is what keeps people sane over a job that runs for weeks.

5. Test older homes first. Homes built before 1978 may have lead paint, and older materials can contain asbestos. Test before anyone cuts or sands.

6. Clean with a HEPA vacuum, not a broom. Sweeping just puts the fine dust back in the air.
