## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What home renovation projects actually require a building permit?

The short version I give clients: if the work touches structure, wiring, plumbing, gas, or the footprint of the building, you need a permit. If it's cosmetic, you almost never do.

Typically requires a permit:
- Structural changes, like removing or modifying a load-bearing wall, or adding beams and headers
- Electrical work beyond replacing a fixture or outlet, such as new circuits, panel upgrades, or subpanels
- Plumbing changes, like moving or adding supply and drain lines, or new fixtures in new locations
- Gas work, including new lines, appliance connections, and often water heater replacements
- Additions, decks above a certain height, and anything that changes the building's footprint
- Roof replacement in many jurisdictions, and HVAC replacement or new installs
- Windows when you change the opening size

Usually does not require a permit:
- Painting, wallpaper, and interior finishes
- Flooring replacement
- Cabinet and countertop swaps in the same layout
- Minor repairs and patching
- Landscaping and small fences (check height limits)

The gray zone is where people get burned. Like-for-like replacements, especially water heaters, often still need a permit because they involve gas or combustion venting. I learned that personally: I swapped a 40-gallon water heater in a rental on a Saturday morning in 2019, assumed it didn't count, and it caused me problems down the line that cost far more than the permit would have.

Three practical tips:
1. Rules vary by city and county, so call your local building department. It's usually free and takes ten minutes.
2. Ask your contractor who pulls the permit. If they suggest skipping it, treat that as a red flag.
3. Unpermitted work can complicate insurance claims and slow down a home sale.
