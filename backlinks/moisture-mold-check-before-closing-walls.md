**Title:** What do you check before closing up a bathroom wall? Mine grew mold 8 months after we finished it

I see a lot of remodel posts here where the drywall goes up the same week the plumbing gets roughed in, so I figured I'd share this.

In March 2021 a tenant in a duplex I own texted me a photo of brown bloom coming out from under the baseboard. We had finished that bathroom eight months earlier. Looking back, we closed the wall without taking moisture readings and without letting the pressure test sit overnight.

Here's what I do now before any wall gets closed:

1. Put a moisture meter on every stud and plate in the cavity. Everything has to read under 19 percent. If one piece reads high, the wall stays open until it dries.
2. Pressure test the supply lines and leave them overnight. A gauge that holds for ten minutes doesn't tell you much.
3. Work out where water would go if it ever got in. Does it have a way out, or does it sit against the bottom plate?

None of this takes long or costs much, and it's a lot cheaper than tearing out a finished wall. What do you all check before closing up, especially in older houses?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I know if a wall cavity is dry enough to close up with drywall?

The short answer: measure it. Don't judge it by how it looks or feels. I learned this the expensive way.

In March 2021 a tenant in a duplex I own texted me a photo of brown bloom coming out from under a bathroom baseboard. We had finished that bathroom eight months earlier. When I opened the wall, the cause was clear: we had closed it without checking moisture in the framing and without a proper pressure test on the plumbing.

These are the three checks I use now before any drywall goes up.

1. Moisture readings on every piece of framing. Use a pin-type moisture meter on every stud, the bottom plate and the top plate in the cavity. Don't just test one spot. Everything should read under 19 percent. Bottom plates and anything near a plumbing penetration are the usual problem areas. If one piece reads high, the wall stays open. Run fans or a dehumidifier and test again the next day.

2. An overnight pressure test on the plumbing. A gauge that holds for ten minutes can still hide a slow drip at a fitting. Pressurize the supply lines and leave them overnight. If the reading drops by morning, find the leak before you close anything.

3. Know where water would go. Ask yourself: if water ever got into this cavity from a failed shower valve, a cracked fitting or a bad tub seal, where would it end up? If the answer is that it sits against the bottom plate with no way to dry out, deal with that now. Use proper waterproofing behind tile and seal your penetrations.

All three checks together take maybe an hour. A tear-out costs days of work, plus the trouble of moving a tenant out while you fix it.
