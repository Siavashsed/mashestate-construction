**Title:** Did anyone else change their project timing because the 25C energy credit cap resets every year?

Something I didn't appreciate until a client walked me through it: the 25C energy efficiency credit, rewritten by the Inflation Reduction Act, has an annual cap, not a lifetime one. Starting in 2023 it covered 30% of qualifying costs, up to $1,200 a year for things like windows, doors, insulation and panel upgrades, plus a separate $2,000 annual bucket for heat pumps.

That changed how I bid. Because the cap resets every January, splitting a project across two tax years can roughly double what a homeowner gets back. A client with a big insulation and window scope could claim up to $1,200 in one year, or phase the work and claim up to $1,200 in each year. There are sub-limits too ($600 a year for windows, $250 per exterior door), so the order of the work matters.

I started asking clients about their tax timing before quoting, which felt strange at first. Some wanted the phased schedule, some didn't care. Worth noting the credit ended for work placed in service after 2025, so today it's mostly a lesson in reading incentive rules before you build a schedule.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How did the 2023 changes to the 25C energy efficient home improvement credit affect when homeowners should schedule renovations?

Short version: the credit became annual, so timing started to matter as much as scope.

I'm a construction project manager, and 2023 was the year this started coming up in my client meetings. Before the Inflation Reduction Act, the 25C credit had a $500 lifetime limit, so most homeowners ignored it. Starting January 1, 2023, it covered 30% of qualifying costs with a $1,200 annual cap for most improvements, plus a separate $2,000 annual limit for heat pumps, heat pump water heaters and biomass stoves.

The annual reset is what matters. Here is how it played out on real jobs:

1. Phasing beat bundling. A client planning insulation, windows and an electrical panel upgrade in one summer would hit the $1,200 cap once. Splitting the work so part finished in one tax year and part in the next let them claim up to $1,200 each year.

2. Sub-limits shaped the order. Windows and skylights were capped at $600 a year combined, exterior doors at $250 each ($500 total), panel upgrades at $600, and a home energy audit at $150. Doing every window in one year left credit on the table.

3. The placed-in-service date is what counts, not the contract date or the deposit. A December job that slips into January lands in the next tax year, which can help or hurt depending on the plan.

4. Product eligibility matters. Windows and doors had to meet specific Energy Star requirements, so we checked labels before ordering.

One update: the credit was eliminated for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so this applies to 2023 through 2025 returns. Confirm the details with a tax professional before you plan around it.
